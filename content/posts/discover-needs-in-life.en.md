Good products usually start from close attention to ordinary life. This is how I look for needs in my own day rather than in a list of trending ideas.

## The core idea

A need never appears out of nowhere. It comes from **friction**, **inconvenience**, and **an expectation nobody met**.

## Four ways I look

### 1. Record the discomfort

When a product or service makes you pause, curse, or work around it — write it down the same day. That small irritation is the signal; the memory of it is not reliable a week later.

### 2. Watch the people around you

Pay attention to what family, friends and colleagues do manually, what they complain about, and which problems they have already normalised. Their workarounds are unpaid product research.

### 3. Track what technology just made possible

New capability reopens old problems. AI, edge computing and cheap sensors have made several things I once dismissed as "nice to have" buildable by one person in a weekend.

### 4. Transfer solutions across domains

A mature pattern in one industry is frequently absent in the next one. Half of "innovation" is noticing the mismatch.

## Validate before you build

Finding a need is the cheap part. Before writing code I want four answers:

1. **Is the problem real?** Talk to at least five people who have it.
2. **What is wrong with the current alternatives?** That gap is your positioning.
3. **Would the target user pay to remove it?** Ask about money, not enthusiasm.
4. **Can I test it at minimal cost?** A landing page, a spreadsheet, one manual delivery.

## A case from my own experience

In university I led a campus healthy-catering project (康韵食尚). It did not start from a business plan; it started from watching what students actually ate and what they avoided. Two hundred questionnaires and more than thirty interviews later, the real gap in healthy catering was not the menu — it was trust in the ingredients and the ordering rhythm around class schedules.

## Summary

Spotting needs is a trainable skill, not a temperament. Stay curious, keep a written record, and keep testing your assumptions — you will find that opportunity is much closer than the pitch decks suggest.
